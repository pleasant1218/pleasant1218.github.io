THE SHORT VERSION

As part of a four-member undergraduate team, I took part in an exploratory microbiology project on how bacteria respond to growth-inhibiting substances in fungal culture supernatant.

Our team combined observations of bacterial growth with an initial investigation of the active material and genetic screening. The work produced preliminary findings and open questions that we brought together in our final project presentation.

My contribution: shared participation in the team’s research work. The project findings below describe our collective work.

01 / BACKGROUND

Why investigate bacterial responses to fungal products?

Our starting observation was that culture supernatant from Trichoderma reesei inhibited the tested Vibrio cholerae strain, while some Escherichia coli strains were less affected under the tested conditions. This raised two connected questions: what material caused the inhibition, and which bacterial factors influenced susceptibility?

Why this was interesting: comparing these responses offered a way to investigate bacterial susceptibility using both chemical fractionation and genetic screening. The aim was exploratory: a difference in growth did not, by itself, explain the mechanism or establish an antibacterial treatment.

The research gap: the active substance had not been chemically identified, and the bacterial determinants responsible for the observed responses remained unresolved.

Culture supernatant is the liquid fraction collected after culturing a microorganism and separating out its cells. We investigated the activity of this material rather than assuming a particular compound was responsible.

02 / RESEARCH QUESTIONS

What caused the inhibition, and what influenced resistance?

  1. Growth responses: how did the tested bacterial strains respond to T. reesei culture supernatant?
  2. Active material: which separated fractions retained inhibitory activity, and what could preliminary analytical tests tell us about the material?
  3. Bacterial genetics: could screening an E. coli transposon mutant library identify a candidate gene associated with altered susceptibility?

03 / EXPERIMENTAL APPROACH

Connecting growth assays, fractionation, and genetic screening

01

Compare bacterial growth

Compare growth with and without fungal culture supernatant across the tested strains, using optical density as a growth readout.

02

Follow inhibitory activity

Extract and separate culture products by C18 chromatography, test collected fractions for inhibition, and investigate active material using preliminary mass spectrometry, dialysis, and a biuret test.

03

Screen bacterial mutants

Construct and screen a mariner transposon library, repeat-test initial candidates, and use arbitrary PCR to investigate the insertion site of a selected candidate.

Scope of the work: the presentation described screening approximately 9,000 colonies. Chemical identification and genetic validation were still incomplete at the time of reporting.

What the experiments showed

05 / INTERPRETATION

Preliminary evidence, with mechanisms still to test

The project connected differential growth responses with activity-guided fractionation and a genetic candidate. These strands helped narrow the next questions, but did not yet show which compound caused inhibition or how gmhB influenced the response.

Limitations & next questions
  • Chemical identity: purify and identify the active material, then test whether a defined compound reproduces the inhibitory effect. Activity in a fraction does not establish that it contains a single active substance.
  • Genetic causality: complete knockout and complementation experiments and distinguish altered susceptibility from a general growth defect.
  • Interpretation of preliminary tests: loss of activity after dialysis and the biuret observation do not, alone, prove molecular size or chemical class.
  • Scope: extend and reproduce comparisons across strains and conditions before generalizing. The work did not establish clinical efficacy or a validated antibacterial mechanism.
