THE SHORT VERSION

For my master’s thesis, I investigated N-acyl taurines (NATs), naturally occurring lipids made from fatty acids and taurine. I explored how they change during liver inflammation and whether two NAT species could reduce inflammatory responses in liver cells.

The project combined a mouse model of inflammation with experiments in human Huh7 cells and primary mouse hepatocytes. The results support a possible role for NATs in regulating inflammation, with further work needed to establish how they act.

THE QUESTION

A marker of inflammation, or part of the response?

I asked two connected questions: how does inflammatory stress affect NAT production, especially when the liver enzyme BAAT is absent? And can C18:1 NAT and C20:4 NAT reduce the response of liver cells to an inflammatory stimulus?

A quick translation: LPS is a bacterial component used here to trigger inflammation. BAAT helps produce NATs in the liver, while FAAH breaks them down.

MY EXPERIMENTAL WORK

What I worked on

01

Liver enzyme assays

I harvested liver tissue and carried out NAT synthase activity assays. I optimized the protein amount and reaction time to measure synthesis capacity within a linear range.

02

Cell experiments

I used Huh7 cells and primary mouse hepatocytes to study the effects of C18:1 NAT and C20:4 NAT before LPS exposure, refining the inflammatory conditions for each model.

03

Gene expression & analysis

I assessed inflammatory responses using RNA extraction, cDNA preparation and qPCR, then compared relative gene expression using GraphPad Prism.

Experimental details & collaboration

Mouse study: wild-type, BAAT knockout, FAAH-S268D knock-in and double-mutant mice received LPS or saline. The analysis connected plasma and bile NAT profiles with liver synthase activity.

Assay optimization: 50 μg liver protein and a 5-minute incubation were selected for subsequent activity assays. These assays estimate synthesis capacity with excess substrate, rather than directly measuring production in a living animal.

Cell study: NAT pretreatment at 50 μM for 1 hour, followed by 3 hours of LPS exposure. The selected LPS concentrations were 0.25 μg/mL for Huh7 cells and 0.05 μg/mL for primary hepatocytes. Expression was normalized to GAPDH/Gapdh using the ΔΔCt approach.

Collaborative contributions: Anna Skab Hassing performed animal handling, plasma and bile collection, and primary hepatocyte isolation. Samuel Addison Jack Trammell performed mass-spectrometric NAT measurements. I carried out the liver activity assays and interpreted these measurements alongside the cell experiments.

Source: thesis methods, pp. 16–21, and assay optimization, pp. 25–26.

WHAT THE RESULTS SHOWED

Three findings I took away

  1. 01

    NAT responses differed between blood and bile

    Plasma NATs rose after LPS across the mouse genotypes, including those without BAAT. Biliary NAT increases depended on functional BAAT. This suggests that the rise in blood cannot be explained by liver BAAT activity alone.

  2. 02

    BAAT supported hepatic PUFA-NAT synthesis

    BAAT-deficient liver samples showed reduced capacity to produce polyunsaturated NATs. Increased Baat mRNA after LPS was consistent with a change in the liver’s synthesis machinery. Other tissue sources remain a hypothesis, rather than a directly measured result.

  3. 03

    Selected NATs reduced inflammatory gene expression

    C18:1 NAT and C20:4 NAT reduced TNF-α expression in Huh7 cells and Tnf-α expression in primary hepatocytes. Primary cells also showed lower Nlrp3 and Casp1 expression. The IL-6 response varied with the cell model and NAT species.

WHAT THIS MEANS

A promising starting point, with questions still open

The results suggest that NATs may help regulate the response of liver cells to inflammatory stress. They do not yet establish a treatment effect in animals or people. Changes in Nlrp3 and Casp1 mRNA also do not, by themselves, demonstrate reduced inflammasome activation.

Limitations & next questions
  • Small saline-control groups limited statistical power in the mouse study.
  • Background NATs in liver lysates complicate the separation of existing lipids from newly synthesized products.
  • Extrahepatic synthesis, including a possible contribution from the kidney, was not measured directly.
  • Further experiments could test protein signaling, additional NAT species, and NAT administration in animal models.
